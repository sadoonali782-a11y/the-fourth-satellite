How your phone finds you

Nobody up there knows where you are.

Satellites don’t track you. They don’t even know you exist. Your phone works out the blue dot all by itself, from a few clocks in space.

Five small steps. Each one is something you can play with.

Satellites just shout the time

Each satellite repeats one message: “I’m satellite A, I’m here, and the time is exactly…” Your phone notes how late it arrives.

3

Your phone’s clock is the problem

Radio is so fast that a clock one millionth of a second out puts you 300 m wrong. A phone’s clock is far worse, so every ball comes out too big by the same amount.

Why there’s a pale circle

Every measurement is a little off. Where the satellites are decides how much that moves your dot.

This part needs JavaScript and 3D graphics.

Pale circle ±1,877 km

Think of it like…

Two torch beams. Cross them at a right angle and they meet in a small, sharp spot, but at a shallow angle they overlap in a long smear. The balls work the same way.

TryPress Bunch them together and watch the cloud of answers stretch out.

TryDrag one satellite to the other side of the sky. The cloud shrinks back.

TryTurn the Earth to look from the side. The specks spread up and down too.

We made every measurement about a million times shakier than real so you can see it. On a real phone the pale circle reaches about 5 m from the dot.2

Height is even shakier than the flat part. Your map only shows the flat part, so the circle lies flat on the ground.

5

Downtown, signals bounce

Tall buildings block some satellites and bounce others. A bounced signal took the long way round, so that satellite seems further away than it is.

Think of it like…

A friend calling your name across a busy street. If the sound bounces off a wall first, it reaches you late and from the wrong side. Your phone gets fooled the same way, and pushes the dot the wrong way.

The whole trick, in five lines

  1. 1Satellites shout the time. Your phone times how late it arrives.
  2. 2Each delay is a distance, and each distance is a giant ball around a satellite.
  3. 3A wrong clock makes every ball too big. A fourth satellite lets the phone find out by how much.
  4. 4Satellites spread across the sky give a small pale circle.
  5. 5Buildings bounce signals, which is why the dot wanders in cities.

Three things that surprise people

Einstein in your pocket

Clocks up there tick faster than clocks down here, by 38 millionths of a second a day. So they’re set to run slightly slow before launch.3

A 12-minute download

Satellites send their orbit details at 50 bits a second. Hearing the full list takes 12.5 minutes, so your phone fetches it from the internet instead.4

Quieter than a whisper

The signal reaching your phone is weaker than the background noise around it. Your phone finds it by knowing exactly what pattern to listen for.4

What we simplified, and sources
  • Step 1’s arc isn’t to scale, but its distance and delay come from a real orbit 20,200 km up.
  • In steps 2 to 4 the satellites stand still, placed by hand to make each idea clear. Real ones circle the Earth twice a day.
  • Step 2 pretends your phone’s clock is perfect.
  • Step 4’s wobble is hugely exaggerated, so you can see it. Its pale circle shows only the flat part of the error, as map apps do.
  • Step 5 has one bounce at most, flat glass walls and a perfect city map. It only works out where you are across the street, and its sky turns about 140× faster than real life.
  • The Earth is a perfect ball, and the satellites’ paths are perfect circles known exactly.
  • Nothing slows the signal down on its way (in real life the upper air adds a few metres).
